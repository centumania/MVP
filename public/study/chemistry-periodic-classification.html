🌐The Big Picture

A 150-year quest to organise the elements: triads → octaves → Mendeleev's mass table → Moseley's number table — then read the trends off the map.
Early patterns→ Mendeleev (mass)→ Modern table (Z)→ Trends predict chemistry
Anchor ideaThe periodic table is a map where position predicts behaviour. Period = number of shells; group = number of outer electrons. Once you can place an element, its size, valency, metallic character and reactivity all follow from two forces: nuclear pull vs shell count.

🏛️1 · Early Attempts

What
Dobereiner's triads: sets of 3 similar elements, middle mass ≈ average of the outer two. Newlands' octaves: every 8th element resembles the first.
Why they failed
Triads fit only a few elements; octaves collapsed after calcium and had no room for new discoveries or noble gases. But both proved a pattern existed — the crucial first clue.
How
Triads: Li(7)-Na(23)-K(39) — (7+39)/2 = 23 ✓. Also Cl-Br-I, Ca-Sr-Ba. Octaves: named after music's 8 notes (sa re ga…).
Where
History-of-science questions — author-idea matching.
Exam
"Triads → Dobereiner; octaves → Newlands." "Octaves worked up to calcium." Triad averages as mini-numericals.

🇷🇺2 · Mendeleev's Periodic Table

What
Mendeleev (1869) arranged the 63 known elements by increasing atomic mass and similar properties (oxide/hydride formulas).
Why the gaps made him great
Instead of forcing elements to fit, he left gaps and boldly predicted the missing elements' properties. When gallium and germanium were found matching his predictions, the table was vindicated.
How
Predicted: eka-boron (Sc), eka-aluminium (Ga), eka-silicon (Ge). Anomalies: hydrogen's position, isotopes, and pairs like Co/Ni & Ar/K where mass order breaks property order.
Where
The most-asked history segment of this chapter.
Exam
"Mendeleev used atomic MASS." "Eka-aluminium = gallium." "Anomalies: H position, isotopes, Co/Ni."

🆕3 · The Modern Periodic Table

What
Moseley (1913): properties are a periodic function of atomic number (Z), not mass. This is the modern periodic law.
Why Z fixes everything
Chemistry is decided by electrons, and electron count equals proton count (Z). Order by Z and the Ar/K & Co/Ni puzzles vanish; isotopes (same Z) share one box naturally.
How
7 periods × 18 groups; 118 elements (92 natural). Metals left, non-metals right, metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) on the zig-zag staircase.
Where
The framework for every question that follows.
Exam
"Modern law → Moseley → atomic number." "7 periods, 18 groups, 118 elements." "Metalloids on the staircase."

📐4 · Periods & Groups

What
Periods (rows): same number of shells. Groups (columns): same number of valence electrons.
Why groups behave alike
Chemistry happens in the outermost shell. Same outer-electron count → same bonding behaviour → a family. That's the entire logic of the table.
How
Read any configuration: 2,8,7 → 3 shells (period 3), 7 valence electrons (group 17) → it's chlorine. Period sizes: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32.
Where
Configuration → position questions, the exam's favourite puzzle type.
Exam
"2,8,2 → group 2, period 3 (Mg)." "H's position is debated (fits 1 and 17)." Period 1 has only 2 elements.

📏5 · Trends I — Size & Metallic Character

What
Atomic size: decreases across a period, increases down a group. Metallic character: the exact opposite pattern of non-metallic.
Why
Across a period, protons increase but shells don't — stronger pull, smaller atom, harder to lose electrons (less metallic). Down a group, a whole new shell is added — bigger atom, looser outer electron, more metallic.
How
Na > Mg > Al (size, across); Li < Na < K (size, down). Most metallic: bottom-left (Cs). Most non-metallic: top-right (F).
Where
"Arrange in order of size/metallic character" — a guaranteed question.
Exam
"Size shrinks across, grows down." "Cs most metallic, F most electronegative." Ranking questions live here.
The two master arrows — every trend question reduces to these.

⚡6 · Trends II — Valency & Reactivity

What
Valency across a period: 1,2,3,4,3,2,1,0 (rises to Si, falls to Ar). Down a group it stays constant. Electronegativity peaks at fluorine.
Why metals and halogens trend oppositely
Metals react by losing the outer electron — easier when it's farther (down the group). Halogens react by gaining one — easier when the nucleus is closer (up the group). Same table, mirror logic.
How
Alkali reactivity: K > Na > Li. Halogen reactivity: F > Cl > Br > I.
Where
Reactivity-ranking questions and the alkali/halogen mirror-trap.
Exam
"Metal reactivity ↑ down; non-metal reactivity ↓ down" — the most-tested opposite pair in the chapter.

👪7 · Group Families

What
Four named families: alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), halogens (17), noble gases (18).
Why noble gases are lazy
Their outer shells are already full — no electron hunger, no reactions, valency 0. Every other element reacts precisely to reach a noble-gas configuration.
How
Alkali: soft, valency 1, violent with water. Alkaline earth: valency 2, less reactive. Halogens: 'salt formers', valency 1. Noble: monoatomic, inert.
Where
Family-property matching; kerosene storage; why NaCl forms.
Exam
"Group 1 = alkali, 2 = alkaline earth, 17 = halogens, 18 = noble." "Halogen means salt-former." "Noble valency = 0."

⭐8 · Key Elements & Facts

What
The static-GK layer: the first 20 elements in order, record-holders, and element name origins.
Why memorise the first 20
Every configuration puzzle (Z → group/period) draws from the first 20. Know them cold and those questions become free marks.
How
H He Li Be B C N O F Ne · Na Mg Al Si P S Cl Ar K Ca. Records: He smallest atom, F most electronegative, Cs most metallic, U (92) largest natural Z.
Where
One-markers on names: mendelevium, polonium (Poland), einsteinium, nihonium (Japan), moscovium.
Exam
"Z=17 → Cl; Z=20 → Ca." "Polonium named after Poland by Marie Curie." Lanthanides/actinides parked below the table.

⚠️Exam Traps & Confusion Pairs

Each has been set in real papers. Read the reality line and it dies.
Trap 1 · Mass vs NumberMendeleev used atomic mass; the modern table uses atomic number (Moseley). Swapping these is the most-set trap.
Trap 2 · Size across a period DECREASESMore protons pull the same shells in tighter. "Size increases across a period" is false.
Trap 3 · Mirror reactivityMetal reactivity increases down group 1; halogen reactivity decreases down group 17. Applying one rule to both is the classic error.
Trap 4 · Fluorine vs Caesium cornersMost electronegative/reactive non-metal = F (top-right); most metallic = Cs (bottom-left). Don't mix the corners.
Trap 5 · Triads vs Octaves authorsTriads → Dobereiner; octaves → Newlands. Octaves broke after calcium.
Trap 6 · Valency is NOT 1–8 acrossIt rises 1→4 then falls to 0: 1,2,3,4,3,2,1,0. "Valency increases up to 8" is false.
Trap 7 · Period = shells, Group = valence electronsReversing the two definitions is a set statement-trap.
Trap 8 · Eka-matchingEka-aluminium = gallium, eka-silicon = germanium, eka-boron = scandium. Options shuffle the three.
Trap 9 · 63 vs 92 vs 118Mendeleev knew 63; 92 occur naturally; 118 known today. Three numbers, three different questions.
Trap 10 · Metalloids listB, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te. Sulphur and phosphorus are NOT metalloids — pure non-metals.
Trap 11 · Noble gases are monoatomicThey exist as single atoms (He, not He₂). "Noble gases are diatomic" is false.
Trap 12 · Same group, not same period, means similarElements with similar chemistry share a group (valence electrons), not a period. Z=9 resembles Z=17, not Z=10.
Assertion–ReasonA: Potassium is more reactive than sodium. R: Its outer electron is farther from the nucleus and easier to lose. → Both true, R explains A.
Assertion–ReasonA: Isotopes caused no problem in the modern table. R: They share the same atomic number, hence the same box. → Both true, R explains A.

🌐The Big Picture

One idea runs through the whole chapter: acids give H⁺, bases give OH⁻, and when they meet they cancel out into a salt.
Acid (H⁺)+ Base (OH⁻)→ Salt + Water· pH measures it
Anchor ideaEverything here follows from two ions. An acid is anything that releases H⁺ in water (sour, turns blue litmus red); a base releases OH⁻ (bitter/soapy, turns red litmus blue). Mix them and H⁺ + OH⁻ → water, leaving a salt. The pH scale just counts how many H⁺ ions are around.

🍋1 · Acids

What
An acid is a substance that releases H⁺ ions in water. It tastes sour and turns blue litmus red.
Why acids need water
A dry acid does nothing — it only shows acidic behaviour when dissolved in water, because that lets it release free H⁺ ions. This is why dry HCl gas doesn't turn litmus red.
How
Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) ionise completely; weak acids (acetic, citric, carbonic) only partly. Acids react with metals to give H₂.
Where
Stomach (HCl), lemon (citric), vinegar (acetic), curd (lactic), car batteries (H₂SO₄), ant stings (formic).
Exam
"Acids give H⁺." "Stomach = HCl." "Vinegar = acetic; lemon = citric; curd = lactic." "H₂SO₄ = king of chemicals."

🧼2 · Bases

What
A base releases OH⁻ ions in water. It tastes bitter, feels soapy, and turns red litmus blue.
Why "alkali" ≠ "base"
A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali. So all alkalis are bases, but some bases (like copper oxide) don't dissolve and aren't alkalis.
How
Strong bases (NaOH, KOH) ionise fully; weak bases (NH₄OH) partly. Bases feel slippery because they react with skin oils to form soap.
Where
Soap & detergents, drain cleaner (NaOH), antacids (Mg(OH)₂), lime (Ca(OH)₂), window cleaner (ammonia).
Exam
"Bases give OH⁻." "Alkali = soluble base." "All alkalis are bases, not all bases are alkalis." NaOH = caustic soda.
Base vs AlkaliAn alkali is just a water-soluble base (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂). "All bases are alkalis" is false.

🌈3 · Indicators

What
An indicator shows, by a change of colour (or smell), whether a substance is acidic or basic.
Why they work
Indicator molecules are themselves weak acids/bases whose structure — and therefore colour — changes with the H⁺ level. That colour flip is our window into the invisible ions.
How
Litmus: acid→red, base→blue. Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, PINK in base. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base. Turmeric: yellow → red in base.
Where
Lab tests, soil-pH kits, why a soap-washed turmeric stain turns red, universal indicator paper.
Exam
"Phenolphthalein = pink in base." "Methyl orange = red in acid." "Turmeric turns red in base." Olfactory indicators change smell.

📏4 · The pH Scale

What
The pH scale (0–14) measures how acidic or basic a solution is. 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic.
Why lower pH = more acidic
pH tracks the H⁺ concentration — more H⁺ means lower pH. Each pH unit is a 10× change: pH 3 has ten times more H⁺ than pH 4.
How
Measure with universal indicator or a pH meter. 'p' stands for 'power' (of hydrogen).
Where
Tooth decay (below 5.5), blood (≈7.4), soil for crops, swimming pools, antacid dosing.
Exam
"pH 7 = neutral." "Lower pH = more acidic." "Tooth decay below pH 5.5." "Blood ≈ 7.4." Each unit = 10× change.

⚡5 · Reactions of Acids & Bases

What
Acids react with metals, bases and carbonates, each giving a characteristic product.
Why neutralisation gives heat
When H⁺ and OH⁻ join to form water, energy is released — so neutralisation is exothermic (the mixture warms up).
How
Acid + metal → salt + H₂ (pop test). Acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation). Acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂ (milky lime water).
Where
Antacids, baking, fizzing tablets, testing gases, metal cleaning (pickling).
Exam
"Acid + metal → H₂." "Acid + carbonate → CO₂ (milky lime water)." "Metal oxides basic, non-metal oxides acidic." Neutralisation is exothermic.
Everyday — the pop and the fizzDrop zinc into acid and the escaping hydrogen makes a burning splint "pop". Drop a carbonate (like an antacid tablet) into acid and it fizzes CO₂, which turns lime water milky. Two gases, two classic tests.

🧂6 · Salts

What
A salt forms when an acid reacts with a base (the metal of the base + the acid radical). Family members: common salt, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder, POP.
Why one salt makes many products
Common salt (NaCl) is the raw material of the chlor-alkali industry: electrolysis gives NaOH, chlorine and hydrogen, and from these come bleaching powder, baking soda and washing soda.
How
NaCl (from HCl+NaOH); baking soda NaHCO₃; washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O; bleaching powder CaOCl₂; POP CaSO₄·½H₂O.
Where
Cooking, cakes, cleaning, water treatment, plaster casts, fire extinguishers.
Exam
Match formula ↔ name (NaHCO₃ = baking soda, Na₂CO₃ = washing soda, CaOCl₂ = bleaching powder, CaSO₄·½H₂O = POP).

🏠7 · Everyday Chemistry

What
Acid–base chemistry explains antacids, tooth decay, acid rain, stings and soil treatment.
Why it's all neutralisation
Nearly every remedy here is neutralising the wrong pH: base for an acid problem, acid for a base problem. Understand that and you can predict the fix.
How
Antacid (base) neutralises stomach HCl. Toothpaste (base) fights mouth acid. Baking soda soothes an acidic ant sting. Lime fixes acidic soil.
Where
Medicine, dental care, farming, environment (acid rain on the Taj Mahal).
Exam
"Antacid = base." "Ant sting (acid) → baking soda." "Acidic soil → add lime." "Acid rain pH < 5.6 (SO₂/NOₓ)."
Why acid rain harms the Taj MahalMarble is calcium carbonate. Acid rain (from SO₂/NOₓ) reacts with it exactly like acid + carbonate — eating away the stone and dulling its shine. It's the same reaction that fizzes an antacid, just very slow and destructive.

💎8 · Crystallisation & Hydrated Salts

What
Many salts crystallise with a fixed number of water molecules locked in their structure — the water of crystallisation. E.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O.
Why colour depends on it
The water molecules are part of the crystal's structure — copper sulphate is blue only because of them. Heat drives the water off and the colour collapses to white; add a drop of water and the blue snaps back.
How
Heating a hydrated salt removes the water (it is not wet — the water is chemically held). The change is reversible: anhydrous CuSO₄ + water → blue hydrated CuSO₄ (also the standard test for water).
Where
Gypsum → POP manufacture, blue vitriol in fungicides, testing whether a liquid contains water, silica gel packets.
Exam
"CuSO₄·5H₂O blue → white on heating." Formulas: gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O, washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, Glauber's salt Na₂SO₄·10H₂O, green vitriol FeSO₄·7H₂O.

pH of salt solutions — statement-question goldStrong acid + strong base → neutral salt (NaCl, pH 7). Strong acid + weak base → acidic salt (NH₄Cl, pH < 7). Weak acid + strong base → basic salt (Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa, pH > 7). The salt remembers its stronger parent.
Dilution safety — always acid INTO waterDiluting acid is highly exothermic. Always add acid to water slowly, never water to acid — water added to concentrated acid can boil instantly and splash acid out.
Strong vs Concentrated — not the sameStrong/weak is about how fully an acid ionises; concentrated/dilute is about how much water it's mixed with. Dilute HCl is still a strong acid; concentrated acetic acid is still a weak acid.
Chlor-alkali — one salt, three productsElectrolysis of brine (NaCl solution) gives NaOH (near the cathode), Cl₂ (anode) and H₂ (cathode). From these come bleaching powder (Cl₂ + slaked lime), and NaOH feeds soap and paper industries — all starting from common salt.

⚠️Exam Traps & Confusion Pairs

Each has been set in real papers. Read the reality line and it dies.
Trap 1 · Litmus directionAcids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. "Bases turn blue litmus red" is false.
Trap 2 · PhenolphthaleinColourless in acid, pink in base. Not the reverse.
Trap 3 · Methyl orangeRed in acid, yellow in base — the opposite of what most people guess.
Trap 4 · Higher pH is more BASICHigher pH = more basic; lower pH = more acidic. "Higher pH means more acidic" is false.
Trap 5 · Base vs AlkaliAn alkali is a water-soluble base. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
Trap 6 · Baking vs Washing sodaBaking soda = NaHCO₃ (bicarbonate, mild, edible). Washing soda = Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (carbonate). Don't swap them.
Trap 7 · Which gas from which reactionAcid + metal → H₂ (pop). Acid + carbonate → CO₂ (milky lime water). Don't mix the gases up.
Trap 8 · Metal vs Non-metal oxidesMetal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic. (e.g. CaO basic, CO₂/SO₂ acidic.)
Trap 9 · Dry acid is not acidicAcids show acidic behaviour only in water (they need to release H⁺). Dry HCl gas does not turn litmus red.
Trap 10 · 'King of chemicals'That title belongs to sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), not HCl or HNO₃.
Trap 11 · Ant sting is acidicAn ant/bee sting injects formic acid, so you treat it with a base (baking soda), not more acid.
Trap 12 · Neutralisation productAcid + base → salt + water (not CO₂). CO₂ comes only from carbonates.
Assertion–ReasonA: Toothpaste is basic. R: It neutralises the acid made by bacteria in the mouth. → Both true, R explains A.
Trap 13 · Hydrated salt is not wetThe water of crystallisation is chemically held in fixed proportion — the crystal is dry to touch. "Hydrated salts are moist" is false.
Trap 14 · Strong ≠ ConcentratedStrong/weak = degree of ionisation; concentrated/dilute = amount of water. Dilute HCl is still a strong acid.
Trap 15 · Salt solutions are not always neutralNaCl → neutral, NH₄Cl → acidic, Na₂CO₃ → basic. "All salts are neutral" is false — the salt follows its stronger parent.
Trap 16 · Acid into water, never the reverseAlways add acid to water slowly. Adding water to concentrated acid can boil and splash — a favourite safety question.
Trap 17 · Gypsum vs POPGypsum = CaSO₄·2H₂O; POP = CaSO₄·½H₂O (made by heating gypsum to ~100°C). Swapping their formulas is a set trap.
Assertion–ReasonA: Acid rain damages marble monuments. R: Marble (calcium carbonate) reacts with acids to release CO₂. → Both true, R explains A.
